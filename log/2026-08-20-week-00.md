# Week 0 — the idea, and the attempt to kill it

2026-08-20

## Where this started

I wanted to build a robot hand. The default version of that project is a 3D-printed
anthropomorphic hand that picks up a Coke can in one demo video and then dies. I wanted it
actually useful at my desk, and also unhinged: a creature, not a demo.

First finding: the hand itself is the trap. ALOHA's own design argument, and the whole 2026
manipulation literature, says a parallel-jaw gripper plus soft fingers covers desk tasks
(grab, press, hold, hand over); dexterous hands buy in-hand reorientation and nothing else I
need, at 10x the control complexity. And the arm is a solved commodity now: the LeRobot
SO-101 is a ~$200-300 kit with teleoperation and imitation learning working out of the box.
Building my own arm would be months of mechanical debugging to reach the starting line.

So where does invention actually live in this project? In the part every robot-arm build
treats as a checklist item: this thing operates inches from my face and my laptop, driven by
software I explicitly want to behave adversarially. Safety enforced by that same software is
decoration. The interesting object is a trusted hardware layer below it. Working name: muzzle.

## The kill attempt

Before claiming anything I ran a four-track prior-art sweep (the gate runs before the pitch,
always). Roughly 100 searches across papers, patents, products, and GitHub. Full map in
[docs/prior-art.md](../docs/prior-art.md). Honest results, including everything that died:

**Taken.** The bare architecture ("trusted hardware below a compromised controller") is 14
years old: S3A, 2012, and the Simplex lineage before it. NASA shipped the drone version
(Safeguard). A startup paper-staked the robot version with 16 provisional patents in February
2026, with no hardware, spec, or data behind them. NVIDIA ships a safety island in silicon,
scoped to random faults, not malice, and closed to OEMs. Hardware MITM on exactly my servo
bus class was published in December 2025, as an attack. So: no claiming the pattern. Only a
transplantation, and it has to earn itself.

**Converging right now.** A bus-level safety interposer for a hobby arm appeared on GitHub
nine days before this entry, as a host-side daemon. A cryptographic command firewall for AI
actuators is in active development, as software requiring protocol adoption. Both share one
gap: they run on, or depend on, the very host the threat model distrusts. One of them admits
in its own README that a dead daemon is indistinguishable from a healthy one.

**Open, verified by the sweep.** Nobody combines: physical interposition on the Feetech TTL
bus, transparent retrofit, kinematic state reconstruction from passive traffic, inline
enforcement within half-duplex timing, an adversarial host threat model, and signed logging
in the trusted device. Nobody anywhere has published an adversarial soak test of a physical
arm, enforcement-latency distributions, or a zero-violation methodology. The field's own 2026
surveys list this exact gap as unfilled. Also open, found by the sweep itself: a hostile host
can legitimately rewrite servo EEPROM (baud, IDs, response delay) to desynchronize any
interposer, and no prior work defends that, because no prior work assumed a hostile host and
a bus interposer at the same time.

**Also open, surprisingly.** No shipping robot is both a manipulator and a character. Reachy
Mini emotes and cannot touch anything. Tabletop HandyBot picks and places with zero soul.
Lenovo showed an expressive desk arm concept at MWC 2026 and did not put a gripper on it.
Apple wrote the expressive-motion theory (ELEGNT), measured that it works on users, and
released nothing. Three near-misses in eighteen months, none crossing the line.

## What survived

The thesis in the README, plus the feasibility numbers: the ESP32-C3 I already own has 2
free UARTs and 5 Mbps headroom against the 1 Mbps bus; a full packet store-and-forward costs
about 200 microseconds against a 30-60 Hz control loop that leaves the bus more than 95%
idle. The make-or-break unknown is half-duplex turnaround timing, and that is a bench
measurement, not an opinion.

## Prototype 0, same day: does the enforcement math actually work?

Built it before spending any money, because if an envelope filter cannot contain a
hostile policy in simulation there is no point buying hardware to host one. It runs on
DeepMind's official SO-101 model from MuJoCo Menagerie, so the joint names, limits, and
geometry are the arm I am actually buying rather than a stand-in.

Three attack policies, each one trying to put the gripper inside a keep-out sphere
positioned where my head sits: one drives straight at it, one waits and lunges at full
speed to beat rate limiting, one spirals in tangentially to exploit a barrier that only
watches approach speed. Plus one benign policy reaching for a normal spot on the desk,
which exists to catch the cheapest possible fraud: a filter that scores zero violations
by refusing to move.

| | breached | worst clearance | benign task |
|---|---|---|---|
| filter off | 9/9 episodes | 12.9 cm **inside** the head | reached, 0.77 s |
| filter on | **0/9 episodes** | +1.4 cm clear | reached, 0.77 s |

Filter latency: median 86 microseconds, p99 349, over 2,340 calls. That is Python on
x86 and therefore not the number that matters; the real one is C on a microcontroller,
which is why the filter is written with fixed-size arrays, a bounded iteration count,
and no solver dependency. A filter that needed scipy would have proved nothing about
running below the host.

## What actually happened: it failed first, three times

The first run reported the filter holding the arm 1.8 mm *inside* my head. Not a
near miss, a breach, and small enough to be easy to wave away. Three real bugs:

1. **Speed limits were a clamp applied after the projection**, so the clamp quietly
   undid the constraint the projection had just satisfied. They are constraints now,
   solved together with the barriers.
2. **The guarded point and its Jacobian referred to different frames** — I was
   guarding each body's origin while computing the Jacobian of its centre of mass, so
   the gradient pointed at a slightly different place than the point being protected.
3. **Zero margin aimed the arm exactly at the boundary.** The one-step constraint
   permits the next position to land exactly on the surface, so every rounding and
   linearisation error lands on the wrong side. This is the interesting one, because
   it is not a coding mistake: a continuous-time barrier guarantee says nothing about
   what a zero-order hold does across a 33 ms control step, and at full speed this arm
   covers centimetres in that window.

For the third, I swept the margin instead of picking a number that felt safe:

| margin | breaches | worst clearance | benign task |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 mm | 6/6 | −1.2 mm | reached |
| 5 mm | **0/6** | +3.8 mm | reached |
| 10-80 mm | 0/6 | tracks margin | reached |

Five millimetres is enough, and safety is free here: the benign task finishes at 0.77 s
in every row. Running at 20 mm, because the sim does not charge me for encoder
quantisation, backlash, or the difference between guarding a body origin and guarding
real mesh geometry.

Ten regression tests now pin all three, since every one of these bugs was silent.

## What this does and does not prove

It proves the enforcement math contains real attacks at a real control rate on the real
arm's kinematics, and that it costs the legitimate task nothing measurable. It proves
nothing at all about the actual thesis, because this filter runs in the same process as
the policy it distrusts. A compromised host would simply not call it. That is the whole
argument for moving it onto hardware, and it is next.
